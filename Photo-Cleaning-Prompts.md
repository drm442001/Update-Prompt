**8 · COMPATIBLE AI TOOLS**
- Adobe Firefly — Recommended; Generative Remove erases dust specks and scratch lines once you paint them into a mask.
- ChatGPT Images — Recommended; it follows instructions that name each scratch by location, so the face stays out of the edit.
- Stable Diffusion — Suitable; masked inpainting with a conservative denoise setting rebuilds grain without redrawing facial features.
- Leonardo AI — Suitable; the Canvas inpaint editor closes long scratches in short passes.
- Flux — Limited; usable only where the host exposes mask-based editing of the damaged area.
- Midjourney — Not recommended; it re-renders the whole photograph, so a person's likeness is not safe.

**9 · HOW TO USE THIS PROMPT**
**How to Use:**
Step 1: Scan or photograph the print at the highest optical resolution the source allows, flat and evenly lit, because a defect is far easier to close when the surrounding grain is resolved rather than smeared by a cheap scanner.
Step 2: Work the damage before the tonality, removing dust, scratches and creases first, since contrast and colour moves made afterwards magnify any patch you left behind and make it visible at print size.
Step 3: Keep every repair inside the original photograph by rebuilding texture from neighbouring pixels, and deliberately leave small honest imperfections such as faint print grain or a soft border, which is what separates a restored scan from a repainted portrait.
Step 4: Customize these variables:
  → `[SURFACE DEFECT TYPE]` = dust specks, hairline scratches, fold creases, tape residue or scanner streaks
  → `[GRAIN PRESERVATION LEVEL]` = fully intact silver grain, lightly reduced grain or clean print surface
  → `[SCAN SOURCE ERA]` = 1900s cabinet card, 1930s snapshot, 1950s studio print or 1970s colour snapshot
Step 5: Generate several variations and compare each against the untouched scan at one hundred percent, checking the eyes, hairline and patterned clothing before you accept anything else.
Step 6: Refine using the negative prompt if required, weighting painted skin and visible rectangular patch edges, then rerun only the region that still shows a repair, and always keep the untouched scan plus a low-resolution test copy, because a bad fill is cheaper to catch at small size.

**Expected Output:**
→ Dust flecks, hairline scratches and the corner crease closed with matched texture so no patch rectangle is visible
→ Authentic film grain retained across the cleaned areas instead of a smoothed, repainted surface
→ Natural contrast with the milky lift reduced and the yellow border cast corrected without modernising the print
→ Expression, face shape and every identifying feature identical to the original photograph
→ An archival master clean enough for reprint, family archives and paid restoration delivery

**Tips & Variations:**
For best results:
• Repair on the flat scan before any resize, since scaling first turns a one-pixel scratch into a soft grey scar that the model tries to preserve.
• Rebuild patterned fabric and lace from the undamaged side of the same garment rather than trusting a generated fill.
• Save the defect mask alongside the result so a client can verify exactly which areas were touched.
Variations to try:
• Variation 1: Add "faint print texture and original border retained" for museum and heritage archive delivery.
• Variation 2: Pair with a crease-heavy torn-edge scan and prioritise edge reconstruction over surface cleaning for framed restorations.
• Variation 3: Combine with the faded colour restoration prompt when a colour print needs cleaning and tonal repair in one workflow.
Tool-specific tips:
• ChatGPT Images: upload the scan and list each defect with its location, because naming the scratch keeps the face out of the edit
• Adobe Firefly: paint a narrow mask around each mark and run Generative Remove without prompt text so the fill stays texture-driven
• Stable Diffusion: inpaint the masked defects with the lowest denoise that closes them, keeping faces outside every mask
• Leonardo AI: split long diagonal scratches into two or three short inpaint passes so a single wide pass never blurs the face
• Flux: use img2img here only if the host caps denoise inside the mask, otherwise the portrait drifts away from the original
